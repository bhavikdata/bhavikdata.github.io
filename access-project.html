Access control analytics for biometric and RFID security

A security operations team had hundreds of access devices producing tens of thousands of entries a day, but no reliable way to see which of them mattered. I cleaned and joined the device data, built checks that flag suspicious access patterns, and gave the team Power BI dashboards they could act on.

The problem

The team had plenty of data but no reliable way to act on it:

  • Access logs came from many device types and sites, with inconsistent formats, duplicate swipes and gaps when devices went offline
  • Security staff reviewed logs manually, so unusual access was often spotted days later, if at all
  • There was no single view of who was entering where and when, across all gates
  • Reporting to management was slow and had to be rebuilt by hand each cycle

What I built

  1. Brought the data together. Extracted entry logs from biometric terminals and RFID gates with SQL and Python, and joined them to user, device and location reference data.
  2. Cleaned and validated it. Removed duplicate reads, standardised timestamps and device IDs, handled missing values and flagged devices with gaps in their logs, which improved data quality by 20%.
  3. Defined what “unusual” means. Worked with the security team to turn their concerns into rules, then added statistical checks on each user’s normal pattern to catch what fixed rules miss.
  4. Flagged anomalies automatically. Every entry is checked against those rules and patterns, and suspicious events are grouped for review instead of left in the raw log.
  5. Built the dashboards. Power BI reports for daily monitoring, anomaly review and device health, replacing the manual reporting cycle.

What the checks looked for

Each flag had a plain reason attached, so reviewers could see why an entry was raised without reading the underlying query.

Out-of-hours access

Entries well outside a user’s usual times or the site’s operating hours.

Repeated failed attempts

Several rejected biometric or card reads in a short window at one device.

Impossible sequences

Entries at gates too far apart to reach in the time between them, or an entry with no matching exit.

Restricted areas

Access attempts to zones outside a user’s normal pattern or permission group.

Device health

Devices that went silent or suddenly logged far more or fewer entries than usual.

Unusual volume

Spikes or drops at a gate compared with the same hour on comparable days.

Results

At 76,000 entries a day, a month holds roughly 2.3 million entries. About 1,200 flags a month means around one entry in every 1,900 needed a closer look — a volume a small team can realistically review instead of scanning the whole log.

Key findings

  • A large share of the noise came from device and data issues, not people. Cleaning the data first stopped duplicate reads and offline gaps from being raised as security events.
  • Rules alone missed behaviour that was only unusual for that particular user, so comparing each person against their own normal pattern mattered.
  • Giving every flag a plain-language reason made the security team far more willing to trust and act on the alerts.

What I’d improve next

  • Score each flag by risk so the most serious events are reviewed first
  • Feed reviewer decisions back in, so rules that mostly raise false alarms can be tuned
  • Move from daily batches to near real-time alerts for the highest-risk checks
  • Add predictive maintenance for devices that show early signs of failing
