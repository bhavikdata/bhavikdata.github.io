Real-time helmet detection from traffic cameras

A computer vision system that spots motorbikes and riders’ helmets in live traffic footage. I fine-tuned two YOLO object detection models on a helmet dataset and compared them on accuracy, speed and how they cope with messy real-world scenes.

The problem

Checking whether motorcyclists wear helmets is still mostly done by people watching roads or reviewing footage. That’s slow and inconsistent, and it doesn’t scale to a whole city’s cameras. The questions I set out to answer:

  • Can an off-the-shelf detection model, fine-tuned on a modest dataset, detect helmets reliably enough in real traffic footage?
  • Is the newer YOLOv8 actually better than YOLOv5s for this task, and what does it cost in speed?
  • How long should the models be trained before extra training stops helping?

What I built

  1. Prepared the data. Used the annotated Helmet Detection Dataset from Roboflow, with bounding boxes for helmets, riders without helmets and related objects. Converted video frames to images and normalised them to YOLO’s input size.
  2. Augmented it. Random cropping, scaling and flipping so the models would handle small, partly hidden and oddly framed helmets.
  3. Applied transfer learning. Started from YOLOv5s and YOLOv8 models pre-trained on COCO, froze the early layers that capture general visual features, and fine-tuned the later layers on helmets.
  4. Trained on a GPU. Ran training in Google Colab at 25, 50, 75 and 100 epochs for both models, tuning learning rate, batch size and anchor sizes.
  5. Evaluated fairly. Compared both models on the same data using mAP, precision and recall, then measured inference time per image.
  6. Tested on real footage. Ran both trained models on traffic surveillance frames and reviewed where each one failed.

YOLOv8 beat YOLOv5s on mAP at every training length. Its inference time was about 9.8 ms per image against 9.3 ms for YOLOv5s, so the accuracy gain costs roughly half a millisecond — still comfortably fast enough for live video.

Key findings

  • More training wasn’t better. Both models peaked at 50 epochs, and mAP fell after that as they started to overfit. Choosing when to stop saved compute and gave better results.
  • YOLOv8 was more confident on the same scene. On the sample frame above it scored every detected bike and helmet higher than YOLOv5s did.
  • Hard conditions separated the models. YOLOv8 coped better with low light, overlapping vehicles and partly hidden helmets. YOLOv5s produced more false positives in crowded scenes.
  • Both models still struggled at night and with occlusion. Missed detections were concentrated in dark frames and where riders were partly blocked.

Limitations

  • The dataset was a single public source, so it doesn’t cover every country, camera angle or weather condition.
  • Training ran on Colab’s shared GPUs, which limited how many configurations I could try.
  • Any real deployment would need a clear approach to privacy and data protection, since the footage shows identifiable people and number plates.

What I’d improve next

  • Collect more night-time, rain and crowded-junction footage to target the weak spots
  • Add thermal or LiDAR data to help in low light
  • Link helmet violations to number plate recognition for an end-to-end compliance report
  • Package the model as a small service that processes a live camera stream
